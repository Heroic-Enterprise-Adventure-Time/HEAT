# 0028: PixiJS draws the camp scene

- **Status:** Decided; amended 2026-10-20 by Issue-89 (the stage fills its box), 2026-10-21 by Issue-99 (heroes at half the scene's scale), 2026-10-21 by Issue-95 (the scale counts device pixels), 2026-10-22 by Issue-15 (the battle scene), 2026-10-22 by Issue-130 (an action's weapon and effect), 2026-11-19 by Issue-205 (a sheet per set, packed by the build, and a run that waits for its art), 2026-11-22 by Issue-341 (a run's cast is who its seed rolls, where the amendment of Issue-205 says "The cast is whoever may appear, not whoever will") and 2026-11-24 by Issue-352 (a battle's road is on its location's ground, where the amendment of Issue-15 says "The ground is the meadow tileset")
- **Date:** 2026-10-20
- **Settles:** Issue-81: what renders the 320×180 scene inside the Angular page, which no document named (raised by the owner in the Issue-80 session).

## Decision

**The scene is drawn with PixiJS 8** (`pixi.js` 8.22.0, pinned like every dependency) inside the Angular page. Its stage is 320×180. The canvas is scaled up by the largest whole number that fits, with nearest-neighbour textures and `image-rendering: pixelated`, so each art pixel is a square of screen pixels. Pixi supplies:

- the textures and sprites;
- the frame loop;
- pointer events on sprites, which is how a facility or a hero is clicked;
- sorting by depth, which draws heroes and facilities back to front by the line they stand on (`docs/process/assets.md`, rule 18).

**What Pixi does not own:**
- The page around the scene. The header bar and the panels a click opens (the heroes hall, a hero's card) are Angular elements over the canvas, so they read at any scale.
- The rules. The scene is handed the heroes and reports clicks; it never calls the hub.
- The layout and the wandering. Where facilities stand and how heroes mill about are plain modules in the client, tested without a renderer.

**PixiJS loads after sign-in.** The camp loads it with a dynamic import, so it is a chunk of its own rather than part of the first page. Measured on the first build (Issue-81):
- the scene's chunk: 310 kB raw, 76 kB transferred;
- Pixi's other lazy chunks: about 270 kB raw;
- the initial bundle: from 269 kB to 311 kB, which is the client's own new code (the camp, the header and the engine's content).

**Particles and sound are chosen when they are needed.** An ability's effect is a drawn frame animation (`docs/design/game.md`, "How the game looks"; PixelLab's effects tool exports frames), and Pixi plays those in its core. Pixi's core has `ParticleContainer` for drawing many particles but no emitter. The emitter port for v8 is a small community package (`@spd789562/particle-emitter`), and whether it is maintained was not checked. Sound is not in Pixi's core: `@pixi/sound` 6 is the line for Pixi 8, and Howler.js is the common standalone. Neither is a dependency yet.

## Amendment: the stage fills its box (Issue-89, 2026-10-20)

**The stage is no longer a fixed 320×180.** A camp is a map of whole tiles, Millbrook's 400×225, and the canvas covers the whole box the page gives the scene. The scale is the largest whole number at which the map's height and its core width fit: the middle 352 px that every facility stands inside, so a box a little narrower than the whole map crops bare ground at its edges rather than drop a scale step. The textures are still nearest-neighbour, so each art pixel stays a square of screen pixels. The canvas shows more ground around the map to cover the rest of the box, with the map centred; a box narrower than the map at scale 1, a phone's, crops the map's edges evenly. `packages/client/src/app/camp/page-layout.ts` holds the arithmetic.

**Why.** With the content area beside the scene rather than over it (`docs/design/game.md`, "The page"), a fixed 320×180 left bars around the scene on most screens. The owner asked for the scene to fill its area. Stretching it by a fraction was considered and not chosen: it blurs pixel art or makes its pixels uneven. Filling with more of the world keeps the pixels square and leaves no bars.

**Not solved by this.** On a screen whose device pixel ratio is not a whole number, such as Windows at 125%, the scale counts CSS pixels, so an art pixel is 1.25 device pixels and the pixels come out uneven. That was true before the amendment too. Closed by the Issue-95 amendment below.

## Amendment: heroes at half the scene's scale (Issue-99, 2026-10-21)

**Heroes in the camp are drawn at half the scene's scale, rounded up:** 1 at scene scale 1 and 2, 2 at 3 and 4, 3 at 5. The scale stays a whole number, so a hero's art pixels stay square; the ground and the facilities keep the scene's scale. The canvas therefore renders at the scene's scale, one canvas pixel per CSS pixel, instead of at the map's pixels enlarged by CSS, and each hero is placed on a whole canvas pixel. A hero still stands by its feet (`docs/process/assets.md`, rule 11), its click area shrinks with it, and it walks 18 of its own art pixels a second, so its stride stays in step with its frames. Battle keeps its own scale, and the sprite files do not change. `heroScale` in `page-layout.ts` holds the rule.

**Why.** At the scene's scale a hero stood half as tall as the hall, and the owner found the heroes too large for the camp. In a mock-up of Millbrook at scene scales 3 and 4, half rounded up read as people in a village; at scene scale 3, heroes at 1, half rounded down, were too small to tell apart or click.

## Amendment: the scale counts device pixels (Issue-95, 2026-10-21)

**The scene's scale is the largest whole number of device pixels per art pixel that fits**, not of CSS pixels, so every art pixel is the same square of device pixels at any device pixel ratio, Windows' 125% and 150% included. The canvas covers its box exactly, with one canvas pixel per device pixel, so the browser draws it without scaling. Where the browser reports the box's exact size in device pixels (`ResizeObserver`'s `device-pixel-content-box`: Chrome and Edge 84, Firefox 93; not Safari, by web.dev and MDN), that size is used; elsewhere the CSS size times the ratio, rounded. A change of ratio, browser zoom or a move to another screen, is followed. The page's regions, side by side or stacked, the menu's labels and its icons, are still decided in CSS pixels; the stacked scene's box takes its height from the scale in device pixels.

**Heroes are half the scene's scale, rounded up, never below one CSS pixel per art pixel and never above the scene.** At ratio 1 that is the rule of Issue-99 unchanged. Without the floor, a phone at ratio 2 with the scene at 2 device pixels would draw its heroes at 1, too small to tap.

**What it changes on screen.** At a whole ratio above 1, the scale moves in steps of one device pixel, not one CSS pixel, so the map may stand a step larger. At a fractional ratio the largest whole step may be smaller than the old uneven one: a 1920×1080 laptop at 125% shows the map at 3 device pixels, 2.4 CSS pixels, where it showed 3 CSS pixels as 3.75 uneven device pixels, with more ground around it.

## Amendment: the battle scene (Issue-15, 2026-10-22)

**The battle is a second PixiJS scene, loaded like the camp's, on first use.** Its stage is 320×180 art pixels, scaled by the largest whole number of device pixels at which it fits its box and at least 400 art pixels of road show across, so a wide box shows more of the world rather than the stage closer (the owner's call), with ground around it, as the camp's map is; heroes and monsters are drawn at the stage's own scale, not half of it, since the battle is about them. The ground is the meadow tileset with a road of its lower terrain across the middle, one column of tiles repeated so that it can scroll. The camp's sprites stand side-on (game.md, "How the game looks"): the party on the left facing east, the other side on the right drawn mirrored, each side's four lines from the middle outwards, a line's units spread in depth and a little aside. `packages/client/src/app/adventure/battle/battle-layout.ts` holds the arithmetic, tested without a renderer.

**It draws from the field and animates from the events.** Where everyone stands and how they are comes from `field.ts`, which reads the adventure's events alone, the same for a run being played and a stored timeline being replayed with no engine. An act plays to its hold frame and holds there until the action resolves, then plays the rest; a hit plays on damage or a harvest; a fall plays and its last frame is held, the tree's crown fading as its provenance says (`docs/process/assets.md`, rule 30); a fallen monster or a felled tree lies a moment and then fades away, while a fallen hero stays down; stepping up a line and a collapse are a walk. An act runs on the adventure's clock, because it holds until its action resolves; everything else runs on the presentation clock, the wall clock times the speed, so fast-forward speeds it all and a fall still plays once the adventure's clock has stopped at the end. The frame counts and hold frames are read from each set's `provenance.json`.

**The progression reads left to right** (the owner's call on Issue-15). The party runs on from the left at the start, at four times the pace, since it is no scroll; on a travel it walks in place while the road scrolls left; at a fight or a gather the other side rushes on from the right as the road scrolls with it, which reads as the party rushing to meet it; what is left of a stage scrolls away on the next travel; a retreat runs off to the left. Every run and every scroll goes at one speed, so the progression never changes pace. The rush on is flavor only: the adventure's clock stops at the entry, the party setting out or a fight or gather beginning, and runs again only when the scene says everyone is on, so the first blow is never struck while the other side is still coming on. The timeline, the log and verification are untouched by it. A resumed run places everyone where they stand, with no rush.

**Without WebGL** the scene does not load, the clock does not wait, and the run plays with the controls alone.

## Amendment: an action's weapon and effect (Issue-130, 2026-10-22)

**An action draws two more sprites, in Pixi's core.** While a unit acts, its weapon is a sprite inside the unit's own container, so it is mirrored and sorted with it; its frames play on the adventure's clock with the act, each holding its own hold frame until the action resolves, and each frame's grip, from the weapon's provenance, sits on the leading hand. When the action resolves, the ability's effect is a sprite of its own, sorted just in front of the unit it landed on, playing once on the presentation clock from the frames of the effect's game pack. Both come from the events alone, so a run and a replay show the same. The weapon by unit type and the effect by ability are one engine module, `packages/engine/src/action-art.ts`, which a crafted weapon later replaces for heroes (game.md, "Heroes"). The frame arithmetic is `packages/client/src/app/adventure/battle/action-sprites.ts`, tested without a renderer. The rules for the art are `docs/process/assets.md`, rules 32 to 35.

**Still no particle emitter.** PixelLab's effects tool bakes its particles into frames, so the battle plays frames and needs no emitter library.

## Amendment: a sheet per set, packed by the build, and a run that waits for its art (Issue-205, 2026-11-19)

**A sprite set reaches the page as one sheet, which the build packs.** The scenes loaded one PNG per frame, 46 requests for a hero set and 918 frames across the sets, read each picture back through a canvas to find the row it stands on, and kept their own caches. The client's art build (`packages/client/scripts/copy-art.mjs`, before every serve, build and test) now packs each set under `art/heroes`, `art/monsters`, `art/props` and `art/weapons` into one image and `sheet.json`: PixiJS's atlas, the frames and the animations they make, and beside it under `set` what a scene reads about the set, the row each clip stands on, measured from the pixels, and how the provenance says it plays. The frames under `art/` stay the one source, as PixelLab exported them; nothing packed is committed, so adding a set is committing its frames; and neither the frames nor the provenance is published. sharp reads and writes the pixels and maxrects-packer places the frames, a pixel apart, so a frame drawn mirrored or split for a fade never samples its neighbour. The step takes about two seconds, and the hero sets' sheets are 360 kB where their frames were 637 kB.

**The image is named for its pixels, and the atlas is read again within a minute.** Everything under `/art` is published to be kept for a year, which holds only for a file whose name changes when it does (Issue-91). A sheet changes whenever a frame is added, the packer's library moves or the atlas gains a field, and an atlas kept from one build with an image from another would cut the wrong frames. So the image is `sheet.<hash>.png`, the hash of its pixels, kept for the year, and `sheet.json`, which names it, is published with the page's own short lifetime (`scripts/publish-client.sh`), as is the facilities' `pictures.json`. A facility's picture is named for its file the same way, `level-<n>.<hash>.png`, and `pictures.json` gives that name beside the row the picture stands on: the review of PR-334 found the row, read again within a minute, beside a picture kept for a year under a name that did not change, so a picture made again would have stood on the wrong row. The script writes everything long-lived first and deletes nothing until the short-lived files that name it are written, so an atlas never names an image the same publish has yet to upload or has just removed. Issue-91 is still owed for the art that is copied as it is, the effects, the ground and the icons.

**The scenes load a set through PixiJS's `Assets`** (`sprite-sheet.ts`), which keeps a sheet by its address and tries a failed load again on the next call, so the caches of loads the scenes kept for units, weapons and the camp's heroes are gone, and so is the readback of pixels: the facilities' base rows are measured by the same build. A set is one asset, its sheet, so no bundle is declared for it. The kinds of set and the clips a scene may ask one for are one list, `sprite-sets.ts`, which the build checks every set against and `loadSpriteSet` types its callers by (`docs/standards/typescript.md`, rule 23). An effect is still its game pack's own sheet and manifest (`docs/process/assets.md`, rule 34), cut where it is loaded.

**The camp's walk is an `AnimatedSprite`,** with `autoUpdate` off and `update` called from the scene's own ticker, so a hero's stride and its steps keep one clock. The battle's unit is not: one sprite there shows five animations, and three of them, the act, the hit and the fall, are placed on other clocks, the act held on the adventure's until its action resolves. That is not `AnimatedSprite`'s model, and one way of choosing a frame for a unit, by its clock, reads better than two.

**A run waits for the art of its whole cast before it begins.** The battle scene was ready once its ground was loaded and loaded each unit's art without waiting, so on a first visit the party ran on unseen. A battle page now hands the scene who the run may show before the run is shown: the party by its sprite sets, and everyone the run may set against it, today exactly whom the job's stages name, and for a replay whom its timeline's battles hold (`cast.ts`). The scene loads the sheets of all of them, their weapons and every ability's effect (`prepare`), and until that is over the run's clock does not move and the scene is not handed the run (`BattleView`). So a new run, a resumed one and a replay each begin with everyone drawn, and nothing is waited for once a run is under way. The scene keeps what a run was prepared with, by set, and a unit takes its picture from there in the frame it appears: asked of `Assets`, even a sheet already loaded is handed over a frame later, and the check on the stack caught a wolf's health bar alone in that frame. The cast is whoever may appear, not whoever will: the owner's word on 2026-11-19, ahead of jobs whose opponents are not all known when a run starts, was that those who might appear are known and few. A unit that appears without having been in the cast loads then and shows when its picture arrives, as every unit did before. A load that fails is over: it is the page's uncaught error, and the run begins without that picture, as it plays with no scene at all where WebGL is missing. So is the whole wait once it has taken eight seconds, so a request that hangs does not hold the run for good, and its picture shows when it arrives (`art-loads.ts`).

**Considered and not chosen:**

- **One sheet for everything.** It would fit: the 918 frames are 3.35 million pixels, inside 2048 by 2048, 12.8 MiB once decoded. Every player would download every set to see six of them, one changed frame would replace the whole file in every browser, and it grows with every set, a hero set being 170,000 pixels. Drawing gains nothing from it at this size: PixiJS batches as many textures into one draw as the GPU has texture units, commonly 16 (`getMaxTexturesPerBatch`).
- **Holding each entry until the art of those entering had arrived,** the party's run on and then the other side's rush on at each fight or gather, as this change was first built. It left a pause in the middle of a run the first time a monster appeared, in which the check on the stack saw a wolf's health bar standing with nobody over it, and it put the wait in three places: the scene, `Play` and the bar. The owner asked for one wait, before the run.
- **Sheets packed by the set scripts and committed,** as Issue-205 was first written. A second copy of every frame in git, a step to remember when a set is added, and a sheet that can fall behind its frames. The owner asked for the build to do it.
- **AssetPack,** PixiJS's own asset pipeline, which packs atlases and also hashes names (Issue-91). Its last release was 2025-11-07, a year before this, on sharp and maxrects-packer versions since superseded, and its dependencies include an ffmpeg installer and font tools; it also could not write the rows a set stands on. The two libraries it packs with are current and are used directly.
- **Our own PNG codec and a row per animation,** which the set scripts already carry on Node's own modules because they may import nothing else. The build may, and reading and placing pictures are common problems with known libraries (`docs/standards/typescript.md`, rule 17).

The amendment of Issue-15 above says the frame counts and hold frames are read from each set's `provenance.json`: since this amendment the build reads them there and the page reads the sheet.

## Amendment: a run's cast is who its seed rolls (Issue-341, 2026-11-22)

The amendment of Issue-205 above has "The cast is whoever may appear, not whoever will", the owner's word of 2026-11-19, given ahead of jobs whose opponents are not all known when a run starts. Decision 0058 then had the engine expand a job's stages from the adventure's seed before the first stage runs, so who will appear is known when the run starts. A new run's cast is now who that expansion names: `jobCast` in `cast.ts` calls the engine's `expandStages` with the adventure's seed, the function the run itself is about to call, so a job that varies loads its wolf's art only for a run that has a wolf. A replay's cast is still whom its timeline's battles hold. A unit that appears without having been in the cast still loads then and shows when its picture arrives, as it may where an adventure another version issued is resumed with this engine. The reasons, and the wider cast as what was weighed and not chosen, are in decision 0058's second amendment.

## Amendment: a battle's road is on its location's ground (Issue-352, 2026-11-24)

**A battle is drawn on the ground of the location its adventure left from,** where the amendment of Issue-15 has "The ground is the meadow tileset": the meadow's for a job of Millbrook's and the crag's for one of Highcrag's, so a party that sets out from a hill fort is not seen on grass. The ground comes with the run, as its cast does: the page hands the scene the location's ground set when it hands over the run, `prepare` loads it beside the cast's art, and `reset` lays the road with it. A location's ground is its camp's layout's (`groundAt` in `packages/client/src/app/camp/scene/layout.ts`), and the ground sets are one list, `GROUNDS` in `camp/scene/ground.ts`, each with the colour its canvas shows until its tiles are laid.

**What it leaves.** Before any run is handed over, the scene is made with the meadow's road, as it was, so a battle of Highcrag's shows the meadow for the moment its art is loading; a replay that cannot be played shows the meadow beside its words. A caravan's road runs from one ground to another, and is drawn on the ground it left until the cart's own issue says otherwise (Issue-353).

**Why here.** The review of this issue's own work found every battle drawn on Millbrook's meadow, with Highcrag's board job, built by Issue-351, among them. Nobody can fight at Highcrag until a caravan lands a hero there (Issue-355), so no stack can show it before then: it is judged on the dev server, with a job of Millbrook's drawn on the crag by a change that is not committed.

**Considered and not chosen:** a scene made with no road until its first run, which would never show another place's ground and changes what every battle shows while it loads, and what an unplayable replay shows, for a moment that only Highcrag has; and a ground named by the job, a second place to say where a job is.

## Considered and not chosen

**A plain canvas with its 2D context**, drawn by the client. It needs no dependency and is enough for a few dozen sprites. The agent first recommended it. The owner prefers a library over code of our own, and Pixi's sprites, animation, pointer events and depth sorting are exactly the code a plain canvas would need written.

**Phaser 4**, which has particles and sound built in. It is a game framework that wants to own the page's loop, its input and its scenes, beside Angular, which owns the page. Particles and sound are not needed yet, and the effects are drawn frames.
